# Data dictionary

Generated by `analytics/build_ledger.py` from `data/raw/Coffe_sales.csv`.
Re-run with `python3 build_ledger.py`. Output is byte-identical across runs
(fixed seed 2006, no wall-clock or network reads).

## What this models

A small cafe does not hold "lattes" in stock. It holds beans, milk and cups. The
source data records drink sales, so every sale is expanded through a recipe into
the ingredients it consumes. One sale of a latte becomes four ledger rows: 18 g
of beans, 220 ml of milk, one 12 oz cup, one lid and one napkin.

This was a deliberate change from an earlier drink-level version. The reason was
a data problem: at drink level, four of the eight drinks sold nothing on most
days (Espresso recorded zero on 74% of days), and a moving average over demand
that sparse is mostly noise. Rolling drinks up into ingredients concentrates the
demand, because milk is consumed by four drinks and beans by six.

The effect on zero-consumption days:

```
Espresso beans          2%      Paper cup 8oz          42%
Napkin                  2%      Drinking chocolate     60%
Paper cup 12oz          2%      Cocoa powder           64%
Cup lid 12oz            2%
Fresh milk              3%
```

Five of the eight ingredients are now dense enough for daily forecasting. The
three that are not are the ones tied to a single low-volume drink.

## Provenance

The source is a coffee-shop point-of-sale export from Kaggle: 3,547 transactions
between 1 March 2024 and 23 March 2025. It records sales only, with no opening
stock, deliveries, wastage or stock locations.

Every row in `stock_movements.csv` carries a `source` column:

- `derived` (16,743 rows) — traces to a real transaction via `sale_id` and
  `caused_by_drink`. The transaction is real. The quantity is a recipe estimate.
- `synthetic` (306 rows) — opening stock, supplier deliveries, expiry write-offs,
  stocktake corrections and emergency top-ups, generated by the simulation.

**Do not delete `source`, `sale_id` or `caused_by_drink`.** Together they are what
makes the provenance claim checkable instead of asserted. Any SELL row can be
traced back to the exact transaction that caused it.

**Licence:** confirm the licence on the Kaggle dataset page before including
`data/raw/` in the submission ZIP. If it is not permissively licensed, ship only
the derived files and reference the source by URL.

## ingredients.csv

One row per stock item. 8 rows.

| Field | Type | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| `ingredient_id` | int | derived | Primary key |
| `sku` | str | derived | `ING-001` format |
| `ingredient_name` | str | synthetic | |
| `unit` | str | synthetic | `g`, `ml` or `unit` |
| `unit_cost` | float | **synthetic** | Invented, for wastage costing |
| `shelf_life_days` | int | **synthetic** | 7 (milk) to 730 (cups) |
| `storage_location` | str | **synthetic** | Chiller, Dry Store 1, Dry Store 2 |
| `lead_time_days` | int | synthetic | 1 for chilled, 3 for ambient |
| `pack_size` | int | **synthetic** | Suppliers sell whole packs |
| `pack_description` | str | synthetic | e.g. `1 L bottle` |
| `reorder_threshold` | int | derived | Static low-stock trigger for the app |
| `reorder_quantity` | int | derived | Suggested order size, whole packs |
| `avg_daily_demand_derived` | float | derived | From the real sales mix |

`reorder_threshold` is the **static** value the web app uses for its low-stock
flag. The ledger simulation uses a separate **dynamic** reorder point recomputed
daily from trailing demand. They are deliberately different; see below.

## recipes.csv

The bill of materials. 36 rows, one per drink-ingredient pair.

Fields: `drink_name`, `ingredient_name`, `quantity_per_serving`.

**Every quantity here is invented.** They are plausible for a small cafe but are
not measurements. This is the main cost of working at ingredient level and must
be stated in the report.

## stock_movements.csv

The append-only ledger. 17,049 rows. Source of truth for stock.

| Field | Type | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| `movement_id` | int | Primary key |
| `occurred_at` | timestamp | When the movement happened |
| `ingredient_id` | int | Foreign key to `ingredients` |
| `movement_type` | str | `RECEIVE`, `SELL`, `SPOILAGE`, `ADJUST` |
| `quantity` | int | **Signed.** Positive adds stock, negative removes it |
| `batch_id` | str | Which delivery batch this touched, for FEFO |
| `expiry_date` | date | Expiry of that batch |
| `storage_location` | str | Where in the store |
| `sale_id` | int | Links a SELL back to the source transaction |
| `caused_by_drink` | str | Which drink consumed this ingredient |
| `sale_price` | float | Price of the drink, SELL rows only |
| `payment_method` | str | cash or card, SELL rows only |
| `reason` | str | Free text, e.g. `Emergency top-up, stock ran out` |
| `source` | str | `derived` or `synthetic` |

**Current stock is never stored.** It is always computed:

```sql
SELECT ingredient_id, SUM(quantity) AS current_stock
FROM stock_movements
GROUP BY ingredient_id;
```

Because `quantity` is signed, that one query is the entire stock calculation.
This is the concrete justification for the immutable-ledger design.

Stock is consumed first-expiry-first-out. To see what is at risk of expiring:

```sql
SELECT batch_id, expiry_date, SUM(quantity) AS remaining
FROM stock_movements
WHERE ingredient_id = ?
GROUP BY batch_id, expiry_date
HAVING SUM(quantity) > 0
ORDER BY expiry_date;
```

## daily_demand.csv and weekly_demand.csv

Modelling tables, derived from `SELL` movements only.

`daily_demand.csv` has 3,104 rows: 388 days times 8 ingredients. **Zero-consumption
days are present as explicit zeros.** Omitting them is the easiest way to get a
moving average quietly wrong, because every average would be computed over trading
days only and come out too high.

Fields: `date`, `ingredient_id`, `ingredient_name`, `unit`, `units`, `weekday`,
`is_weekend`, `month`.

`weekly_demand.csv` has 448 rows, weeks starting Monday. Fields: `ingredient_id`,
`ingredient_name`, `unit`, `week_starting`, `units`.

Mean daily consumption: milk 1,040 ml, beans 106 g, 12 oz cups 8, napkins 9,
chocolate 18 g, cocoa 15 g, 8 oz cups 1.

**Forecast daily for milk, beans, 12 oz cups, lids and napkins.** Those five have
enough density. **Forecast weekly for 8 oz cups, drinking chocolate and cocoa
powder**, or note in the report that they are too sparse for a reliable daily
forecast, which is itself a legitimate finding.

## Simulation notes

The reorder policy went through three versions. The failures are worth reporting
because the validation step caught each one, and the brief says an honestly
diagnosed failure still earns credit.

1. **Flat average threshold.** 343 sales hit empty shelves. Demand roughly
   doubles across the year, from 175 units in March 2024 to 426 in October, so a
   threshold from the whole-period average undershoots in busy months.
2. **Flat threshold inflated 1.8x.** Fixed the shortfalls but pushed spoilage to
   21.8% of units, because it over-orders in quiet periods.
3. **Trailing 28-day demand, adopted.** Reorder point recomputed daily from the
   last 28 days, with a three standard deviation safety buffer. Order size is
   capped at half the item's shelf life in days of demand, so a perishable is
   never bought in a quantity it cannot sell through.

Final behaviour: 6 stockout events requiring an emergency top-up, and 8.6% of
milk written off as expired.

**Two numbers to use as baselines in the report.** The 6 stockouts are occasions
the shop ran to the cash-and-carry and paid retail. The 8.6% milk wastage is
roughly 34.7 litres over the year. Frame the forecasting feature as the thing
that reduces both, and you have a success criterion that is a business outcome
rather than just a low error metric.

## Limitations

State these in the report.

**Every recipe quantity is invented.** The sales are real; the claim that a latte
uses 220 ml of milk is not. Any conclusion about ingredient consumption inherits
that assumption. A real deployment would calibrate recipes against actual purchase
records.

**Only milk ever spoils.** It is the sole item with a short shelf life, so the
expiry-tracking feature is in practice a milk feature on this dataset. That is
realistic for a cafe but means the feature is less exercised than the row count
suggests.

**Shelf lives, pack sizes, costs and storage locations are invented.** None exist
in the source data.

**Deliveries and wastage are simulated.** A forecast evaluated against this data
is partly being scored on its ability to predict a process whose rules are known.
Expect real-world performance to be worse.

**Seven calendar days have no transactions**, including a three-day gap from 3 to
6 May 2024. These appear as zero-demand days. Whether the shop was closed or the
data is incomplete cannot be determined from the source.

**One row has an hour mismatch** between the `Time` field and the `hour_of_day`
column in the source. The timestamp is treated as authoritative.

**Prices vary slightly per drink** (four distinct values each), most likely a
promotion or price change. This has no effect on unit forecasting.
